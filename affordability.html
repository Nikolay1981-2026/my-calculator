How Lenders Calculate What You Can Afford

When you apply for a mortgage, lenders don't just look at your income. They look at your Debt-to-Income ratio (DTI). This is the percentage of your gross monthly income that goes toward paying debts.

The 43% DTI Rule

Most lenders follow the Qualified Mortgage (QM) rule, which states that your total monthly debts (including the new mortgage, car loans, and credit cards) should not exceed 43% of your gross monthly income. If your DTI is higher than 43%, it will be very difficult to get approved for a conventional loan.

Example: If you earn $8,000 a month, 43% is $3,440. If you already pay $500 for a car loan, your max housing payment (mortgage + taxes + insurance) can be up to $2,940.

How to Increase Your Buying Power

  • Pay off debts: Paying off a $300 car loan can increase your mortgage approval by tens of thousands of dollars.
  • Save a bigger down payment: A larger down payment means a smaller loan, which lowers your monthly payment and improves your DTI.
  • Improve your credit score: A higher score gets you a lower interest rate, which lowers your monthly payment.

Affordability — FAQ

What is a good DTI ratio?

A DTI under 36% is considered excellent. A DTI between 36% and 43% is acceptable for most lenders. Anything above 43% is considered risky.

Should I include my utilities in the DTI?

No. Utilities (electricity, water, internet) are not included in your DTI calculation. Only debt obligations (loans, credit cards, child support) are counted.

What if I have no credit score?

If you have no credit history, some lenders offer "alternative credit" programs. They may look at your rent payment history, utility bills, and phone bills to approve you for an FHA loan.
